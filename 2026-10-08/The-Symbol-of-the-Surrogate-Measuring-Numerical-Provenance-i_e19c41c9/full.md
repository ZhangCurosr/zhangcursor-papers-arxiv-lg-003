# The Symbol of the Surrogate: Measuring Numerical Provenance in Neural PDE Solvers

Ridham Patel

Computer Science & Engineering Indian Institute of Technology Gandhinagar Gandhinagar, India ridham.patel@iitgn.ac.in

## Abstract

Neural PDE surrogates are trained on numerical solver outputs that contain both physical evolution and solver-specific discretization errors. Because surrogates are also evaluated against held-out trajectories from the same solver, standard benchmarks cannot distinguish fidelity to the exact evolution from imitation of the numerical scheme. We introduce an empirical Fourier-symbol diagnostic that probes a trained surrogate’s linearized one-step operator with individual Fourier modes and compares it with both exact-evolution and training-scheme references. To address architectural spectral bias, we train identical networks on schemes with orthogonal dissipative and dispersive signatures and compare their learned operators. In linear advection, the learned surrogates reproduce the training schemes amplitude and phase errors, with the twin-scheme difference reaching more than 99.8% of the analytically predicted full-imitation ceiling. The same behavior occurs for a non-local Fourier neural operator and at the operator level for nonlinear Burgers dynamics. These results show that agreement with solver-generated test data does not by itself establish fidelity to the exact evolution. Fourier-symbol measurements provide a direct diagnostic of numerical provenance.

## 1 Introduction

Neural surrogates for partial differential equations (PDEs) are often trained on trajectories produced by a numerical solver and, in the prevailing protocol, evaluated against held-out trajectories from the same solver. Benchmarks are constructed this way as a matter of course: PDEBench and PDEArena distribute solver-generated trajectories as targets, and standard neural-operator and autoregressive simulator studies evaluate against numerical rollouts (Takamoto et al., 2022; Gupta and Brandstetter, 2022; Li et al., 2021; Brandstetter et al., 2022; Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020).

Solver output is not the physical evolution. Every consistent scheme introduces a discretization error whose wavenumber dependence is classically characterized: first-order upwind adds artificia diffusion that damps high wavenumbers, while centered and Crank–Nicolson discretizations of advection conserve amplitude but propagate high wavenumbers at the wrong speed (LeVeque, 2007). A surrogate trained on such data is therefore exposed to two superimposed signals, the physical evolution and the solver’s numerical signature, and the training objective does not separate them.

This creates an ambiguity that the standard protocol cannot resolve. If the test targets are generated by the same scheme as the training targets, then the benchmark rewards agreement with the scheme, and a surrogate that has reproduced the scheme’s numerical signature can score as well as, or better than, a surrogate that has learned the underlying evolution. A low error against held-out solver trajectories therefore does not establish fidelity to the exact evolution; it establishes fidelity to the solver, of which fidelity to the exact evolution is only one possible cause. This ambiguity is adjacent to prior work on solver-aware learning, low-fidelity supervision and learned correction of numerical solver errors (Suriyanarayanan et al., 2026; Koehler and Thuerey, 2025; Um et al., 2020; Howard et al., 2023).

The closest spectral diagnostic does not close this gap. Gao et al. (2026) measure how far a learned operator sits from the exact operator, which quantifies error but does not attribute it: the two candidate targets, the exact evolution and the training scheme, are never compared side by side. What is missing is an instrument that reports which of the two a learned operator is near.

We use the surrogate’s empirical Fourier symbol as that instrument. Following the single-mode probing primitive of Gao et al. (2026), we apply the trained surrogate to one Fourier mode and read the corresponding output coefficient in one forward pass, with no gradients. The same object exists in closed form for both references: $G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k )$ for the exact evolution and $G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ( k )$ , the von Neumann amplification factor, for the scheme. Provenance then becomes a projection: a scalar $\alpha ( k )$ that is 1 when the learned symbol coincides with the scheme and 0 when it coincides with the exact reference.

One confound stands in the way of reading α directly. Neural networks are biased toward low frequencies for architectural reasons (Rahaman et al., 2019; Tancik et al., 2020; Khodakarami et al., 2026), and that bias damps high wavenumbers, which is the same qualitative effect a dissipative scheme has. A single surrogate trained on a dissipative scheme would report $\alpha > 0$ even if it had learned no scheme-specific behaviour at all.

Our identification strategy removes this confound by construction. We train identical networks on two schemes whose errors are orthogonal (upwind, whose error is almost purely dissipative, and Crank–Nicolson, whose error is purely dispersive) on one shared mesh and time step, with every other experimental factor held fixed (LeVeque, 2007). Under those conditions, the schemeindependent component of the architectural contribution is suppressed in the difference of the two learned symbols. Target-dependent approximation and optimization effects need not cancel, while the scheme contributions separate onto orthogonal axes. Spectral bias alone predicts a twin-scheme difference of zero; scheme imitation predicts a specific, analytically computable value.

The measurements that follow are consistent with scheme imitation and not with the alternatives. On linear advection the twin-scheme difference recovers over 99.8% of its full-imitation ceiling on both axes; the same holds for a non-local Fourier neural operator that demonstrably fits the exact evolution when trained on exact data; on nonlinear Burgers the learned operator remains aligned with its training scheme by $5 { - } 2 7 \times$ on its own states, including under common-input evaluation; and fine-tuning on 25 exact samples moves α to approximately 0 without removing the architecture-dependent accuracy floor.

We make three contributions.

• We turn the empirical Fourier symbol of a trained surrogate, a measurement primitive introduced for operator networks by Gao et al. (2026), into a provenance test, by resolving the symbol into dissipation and dispersion coordinates and projecting it onto the axis joining the exact reference to the training scheme.

• We introduce twin-scheme differencing, an identification strategy in which two identical networks trained on schemes with orthogonal error signatures suppress the common architectural contribution, giving a falsifiable prediction that spectral bias alone cannot reproduce.

• We apply the diagnostic to standard surrogates and find scheme-consistent symbols for a local CNN and a non-local FNO on linear advection, at the operator level on nonlinear Burgers with the input distribution held fixed, and we show that provenance and accuracy respond on different scales under high-accuracy fine-tuning.

## 2 Method

## 2.1 Problem setup

We consider a periodic PDE on $[ 0 , L )$ discretized on N equispaced points with spacing $\Delta x = L / N$ and a fixed time step $\Delta t .$ Three one-step maps on the discrete state $\mathbf { \Psi } _ { u } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { N }$ are relevant:

• the exact reference $\mathcal { E } ,$ , the true evolution over $\Delta t$ sampled on the grid;

• the scheme reference $\boldsymbol { S } ^ { ( s ) }$ , one step of numerical scheme s;

• the learned operator $f _ { \theta } ,$ , a network trained on pairs $( u , \mathcal { S } ^ { ( s ) } u )$ generated by scheme s.

Training data are generated by $\boldsymbol { S } ^ { ( s ) }$ and standard evaluation compares $f _ { \theta }$ to $\boldsymbol { S } ^ { ( s ) }$ on held-out inputs. The question we ask of $f _ { \theta }$ is different: is the operator it learned closer to $\mathcal { E }$ or to $\boldsymbol { S } ^ { ( s ) }$ , and by how much? Appendix A gives a schematic of the measurement pipeline.

## 2.2 The empirical Fourier symbol

On a periodic grid, a linear one-step map that commutes with translations acts on each discrete Fourier mode by multiplication by a complex number: its symbol (LeVeque, 2007; Gao et al., 2026). We measure that number for a trained surrogate directly. For each resolved wavenumber $k \in \{ 1 , \ldots , N / 2 - 1 \}$ we build the probe field

$$
u _ { j } ^ { ( k ) } = \varepsilon \cos ( 2 \pi k x _ { j } / L ) , \qquad j = 0 , \ldots , N - 1 ,\tag{1}
$$

apply the surrogate’s one-step map once, take the discrete Fourier transform of the output, and divide its k-th coefficient by the k-th coefficient of the input:

$$
\hat { G } _ { \theta } ( k ) = \frac { \left[ \mathcal { F } f _ { \theta } ( u ^ { ( k ) } ) \right] _ { k } } { \left[ \mathcal { F } u ^ { ( k ) } \right] _ { k } } , \qquad \varepsilon \to 0 .\tag{2}
$$

This costs one forward pass per wavenumber and requires no gradients and no access to model internals. We use $\varepsilon = 1 0 ^ { \sp { - 2 } }$ throughout.

Equation (2) is a symbol only if the probe stays in the linear regime, so we certify rather than assume it. Sweeping $\varepsilon \in [ 1 0 ^ { - 4 } , 1 ]$ , the recovered $\hat { G } _ { \theta }$ is ε-independent to $1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 }$ in relative terms across $\varepsilon \in [ 1 0 ^ { - 4 } , \bar { 1 } 0 ^ { - 1 } ]$ and departs only at $\varepsilon \sim 1$ , where the GELU nonlinearity (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016) is no longer probed locally (Appendix L). Applied to the numerical solvers themselves, the same procedure returns their analytic symbols to $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 6 }$ , which fixes the zero of the instrument.

For nonlinear problems, the one-step map is not generally a Fourier multiplier. Around a nonconstant base state u¯, the Jacobian $D f _ { \theta } { \vert } _ { \bar { u } }$ need not commute with translations, so a single Fourier input can generate responses in other modes. In this case (2) should be read as the same-mode, diagonal Fourier response $\langle e _ { k } , D f _ { \theta } | _ { \bar { u } } e _ { k } \rangle / \langle e _ { k } , e _ { k } \rangle$ , not as a full scalar symbol. We take u¯ from the solver’s own forced steady states, the distribution the surrogate was trained on. This matches the frozen-coefficient viewpoint behind linearized von Neumann analysis of nonlinear schemes (LeVeque, 2007): both operators are queried about the same occupied state rather than about an unrelated distribution.

## 2.3 The two reference symbols

Linear advection $u _ { t } + c u _ { x } = 0$ makes the two references explicit. Write $\sigma = c \Delta t / \Delta x$ for the Courant number and $\varphi _ { k } = 2 \pi k / N$ for the phase advance per cell of mode k. The exact evolution translates each mode without changing its amplitude,

$$
G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k ) = e ^ { - i \sigma \varphi _ { k } } , \qquad | G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } | = 1 ,\tag{3}
$$

whereas a consistent scheme applies a different multiplier, known in closed form. For the two schemes used throughout,

$$
G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { u p } } ( k ) = 1 - 2 \sigma \sin ^ { 2 } \frac { \varphi _ { k } } { 2 } - i \sigma \sin \varphi _ { k } , \qquad G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { C N } } ( k ) = \frac { 1 - i a _ { k } / 2 } { 1 + i a _ { k } / 2 } , \quad a _ { k } = \sigma \sin \varphi _ { k } ,\tag{4}
$$

for explicit first-order upwind and Crank–Nicolson with centered differences, respectively (LeVeque, 2007). The two schemes err in complementary ways. Upwind damps: $| G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { { u p } } } | < 1 \dot { }$ , and at the Courant number used here, $\sigma = 1 / 2$ , it factors as $G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { u p } } = e ^ { - i \varphi _ { k } / 2 } \cos ( \varphi _ { k } / 2 )$ , so its phase agrees with $G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } }$ exactly and its entire error is a modulus deficit. Crank–Nicolson is unitary, $| G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { C N } } | = 1$ and its entire error is a phase error. At this Courant number the two scheme errors therefore separate exactly into a modulus component and a phase component, which is the property the identification strategy of Sec. 2.6 exploits; away from $\sigma = 1 / 2$ the upwind error acquires a phase component as well and the separation becomes approximate.

## 2.4 Dissipation and dispersion coordinates

Because $| G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } | = 1$ , multiplying by the complex conjugate $\overline { { G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } } }$ rotates the complex plane so that the exact reference sits at 1 and the error $\hat { G } _ { \theta } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } }$ decomposes into a radial and a tangential part:

$$
\begin{array} { r l } { \Delta _ { \mathrm { d i s s } } ( k ) = \mathrm { R e } \left[ ( \hat { G } _ { \theta } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ) \overline { { G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } } } \right] } & { { } \approx \ : | \hat { G } _ { \theta } | - 1 , } \\ { \Delta _ { \mathrm { d i s p } } ( k ) = \mathrm { I m } \left[ ( \hat { G } _ { \theta } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ) \overline { { G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } } } \right] } & { { } \approx \ : \mathrm { a r g } \ : \hat { G } _ { \theta } - \mathrm { a r g } \ : G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } . } \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

The left-hand sides are the quantities we report; the right-hand approximations hold to first order in the deviation and give them their names. $\Delta _ { \mathrm { d i s s } }$ is an amplitude error: negative means the mode is damped relative to the exact evolution. $\Delta _ { \mathrm { d i s p } }$ is a phase error: positive means the mode is under-rotated, i.e. it propagates more slowly than the true wave, which is the classical lagging-phase error of a centered discretization. Modified-equation theory places each scheme on one of these axes: upwind, whose leading error term is an even derivative, is radial; Crank–Nicolson, whose leading error term is odd and which is unitary, is tangential (LeVeque, 2007).

## 2.5 The provenance coefficient

All the quantities needed to attribute the learned symbol are now defined. In the rotated frame the exact reference sits at the origin of the error plane and the training scheme sits at $G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } }$ We report where the learned symbol falls along the line joining them:

$$
\alpha ( k ) ~ = ~ { \frac { \mathrm { R e } \left[ ( \hat { G } _ { \theta } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ) { \overline { { ( G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ) } } } \right] } { \left| G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } \right| ^ { 2 } } } .\tag{6}
$$

This is the least-squares projection of the learned error onto the scheme error direction, treating $\mathbb { C }$ as $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ ; the conjugate is required for the numerator to be a real inner product. Three values orient the scale:

$\alpha = 1$ : the learned symbol has the same component along the scheme-error direction as the training scheme. This does not rule out an additional component orthogonal to that direction.

$\alpha = 0 \colon$ the learned symbol is consistent with the exact reference in the scheme-error direction. It does not by itself certify that the network has learned the physics; it says the scheme’s error direction is absent from the learned symbol.

$\alpha < 0 \mathrm { : }$ the learned symbol lies beyond the exact reference in the direction opposite to the scheme, the regime in which an emulator is more accurate than the solver that trained it (Koehler and Thuerey, 2025).

The corresponding orthogonal residual is

$$
r _ { \perp } ( k ) = \Big | \hat { G } _ { \theta } ( k ) - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k ) - \alpha ( k ) \big ( G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ( k ) - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k ) \big ) \Big | .\tag{7}
$$

For the linear experiments, the modulus and phase summaries together with Figure 1 show that this residual is negligible relative to the scheme displacement.

## 2.6 Twin-scheme identification

A single value of α is not sufficient evidence, because the learned symbol carries an architectural contribution as well as a scheme contribution. Model the symbol learned from scheme s as

$$
\hat { G } _ { \theta } ^ { ( s ) } ( k ) = G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k ) + \alpha _ { s } ( k ) \bigl [ G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { ( s ) } ( k ) - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k ) \bigr ] + B ( k ) + \eta ,\tag{8}
$$

where $\alpha _ { s } ( k )$ is the scheme-imitation coefficient that (6) is meant to estimate, $B ( k )$ is the architectureintrinsic spectral bias (the displacement the network would exhibit from its inductive bias and optimization alone), and $\eta$ collects seed-to-seed noise. Applying (6) to $\hat { G } _ { \theta } ^ { ( s ) }$ returns $\alpha _ { s } ( k )$ plus the projection of $B ( k )$ onto the scheme-error direction, so the estimator is unbiased only when that projection vanishes. It does not vanish for a dissipative scheme: spectral bias damps high wavenumbers, so $B ( k )$ points radially inward, and so does the upwind error. A network with

$B ( k ) \neq 0$ that had learned nothing scheme-specific would therefore still report $\alpha _ { \mathrm { u p } } > 0$ . This is the confound a single-scheme study cannot escape.

Two features of the design break the tie. First, $G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { C N } } - G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } }$ is tangential and therefore orthogonal to a radial $B ( k )$ , so α<sub>CN</sub> is not contaminated by spectral bias. Second, and more robustly, if the architecture, mesh, time step, input distribution, optimizer, schedule, sample count and seed sequence are held fixed across the two schemes, then the scheme-independent component of $B ( k )$ is suppressed in the difference of the two learned symbols. Target-dependent approximation and optimization effects need not cancel, so this is an identification strategy rather than an exact causal decomposition. We therefore report

$$
\Delta _ { \mathrm { t w i n } } ( k ) = \left[ \hat { G } _ { \theta } ^ { \mathrm { u p } } ( k ) - \hat { G } _ { \theta } ^ { \mathrm { C N } } ( k ) \right] \overline { { { G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k ) } } } ,\tag{9}
$$

where $G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } ( k )$ is again the complex conjugate, so that the real and imaginary parts of $\Delta _ { \mathrm { t w i n } }$ are the twin difference in the dissipation and dispersion coordinates of (5). Under $\alpha _ { \mathrm { u p } } = \alpha _ { \mathrm { C N } } = 1$ and exact cancellation of $B , ( 8 )$ gives afull-imitation ceiling that is computable from the analytic symbols alone, $\Delta _ { \mathrm { t w i n } } ^ { \mathrm { m a x } } = ( G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { u p } } \bar { - } G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ^ { \mathrm { C N } } ) \overline { { G _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } } }$ , which supplies the scale against which a measured $\Delta _ { \mathrm { t w i n } }$ is meaningful.

The geometry gives the design its falsifiable content. Because the upwind error is radial and the Crank– Nicolson error tangential, scheme imitation displaces the two learned symbols along orthogonal directions, forming a right angle in the $( \Delta _ { \mathrm { d i s s } } , \Delta _ { \mathrm { d i s p } } )$ plane (exactly so while the deviations are small, approximately so at the largest wavenumbers, where the Crank–Nicolson phase error approaches 1 radian and its symbol curves along the unit circle), and produces a $\Delta _ { \mathrm { t w i n } }$ with both components nonzero and matching the ceiling. Spectral bias alone predicts both symbols displaced radially, no tangential separation, and $\Delta _ { \mathrm { t w i n } } = 0$ in both components however large the individual displacements are. Consistency with the exact reference predicts both symbols at the origin. Appendix B tabulates these predictions explicitly.

## 3 Experimental setup

The identification argument requires the solver to be the only source of variation, so within each experiment the architecture, initialization seed sequence, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, batch size, epoch count, sample count, mesh, time step and distribution of input fields are identical across corpora; only the solver that generated the targets differs.

For linear advection $( u _ { t } + u _ { x } = 0 ~ \mathrm { o n } ~ [ 0 , 1 ] , \ : N = 6 4 , \sigma = 0 . 5 )$ we generate three corpora of 4000 input/target pairs from random band-limited fields with approximately flat spectra: upwind, Crank–Nicolson, and an exact spectral control. Surrogates are a periodic five-layer CNN and, for the architecture control of Sec. 4.2, an FNO retaining all $\mathrm { { \bar { \it { N } } / 2 } }$ resolved modes (Li et al., 2021); both are trained with Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) and cosine annealing (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) over 400 epochs and three seeds. For forced viscous Burgers $( u _ { t } + u u _ { x } = \nu u _ { x x } + f $ on [0, 2π], $N = 1 2 8$ $\nu = 0 . 0 4 )$ we use three method-of-lines RK4 solvers that share mesh, time step, viscosity and forcing and differ only in the convection discretization: dealiased spectral (used as the high-fidelity reference), first-order upwind, and second-order centered (LeVeque, 2007; Boyd, 2001). Surrogates learn the $M = 1 5$ substep map. Full architectures, hyperparameters, data-generation details and the seed protocol are given in Appendix K.

## 4 Results

## 4.1 Linear advection: direct evidence of numerical provenance

The question this experiment answers is whether the operator learned from solver-generated data carries the training scheme’s numerical signature, and whether that signature can be distinguished from architectural spectral bias. ## 4.2 A non-local architecture: ruling out locality and capacity

The locality observation suggests an alternative explanation for Sec. 4.1: a CNN represents local finitedifference stencils easily, and the two schemes are local finite-difference stencils, so the agreement might reflect the architecture’s hypothesis class rather than anything about training on solver data. Under that explanation the effect should weaken or disappear for an architecture that can represent the exact evolution.

We therefore repeat the twin-scheme experiment with an FNO (Li et al., 2021), which is non-local by construction and retains all $N / 2$ resolved modes. Its spectral convolution has the Fourier degrees of freedom needed for the exact advection multiplier, and the exact-trained run serves as the empirical capacity check (Appendix D). Corpora, optimizer, schedule, sample count and seeds are unchanged.

## 4.3 Nonlinear Burgers: provenance beyond linear dynamics

Linear advection admits an exact symbol for every operator involved, which is what makes it the cleanest setting; the question here is whether numerical provenance survives when the dynamics are nonlinear and the one-step map is not a Fourier multiplier. On forced viscous Burgers the three solvers show the expected stationary-spectrum separation: the fraction of energy above $k = N / 4$ is $8 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 }$ for upwind, $3 . 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ for the physical spectral solver and $1 . 3 \times \mathrm { 1 0 ^ { - 5 } }$ for centered. There is therefore a scheme signature present to be imitated.

We evaluate the surrogate’s one-step operator action on the solver’s own forced steady states, with no rollout, and compare its output spectrum both to the solver’s true next-state spectrum $( D _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } )$ and to the dealiased spectral reference applied to the same inputs $( D _ { \mathrm { e x a c t } } )$ , each measured as the mean |∆ log $E ( k ) |$ over the upper spectrum.

Table 3 shows the same preference found in the linear case, on an operator that is not a linear stencil: each surrogate reproduces its scheme’s spectral action while diverging from the dealiased spectral reference, and the discretization error it could imitate is large relative to that agreement.

We also repeat the comparison with a common input distribution, so that only the operator varies (Appendix H). The learned operator remains substantially closer to its training scheme’s action than to the dealiased spectral reference action: 50.2× for upwind and Two additional checks clarify the scope of the diagnostic. First, autoregressive Burgers rollout adds a scheme-independent over-smoothing that dominates the attractor-level spectrum, so provenance must be measured at the operator level (Appendix I). Second, fine-tuning a scheme-trained CNN on exact-evolution samples moves α from 1 to approximately 0 with as few as 25 samples, while exact-reference MSE remains at the architecturedependent floor (Appendix J). Thus provenance and predictive error are distinct quantities that can respond on different scales.

## 5 Conclusion

Solver-generated training data carry numerical provenance: the discretization error of the scheme that produced them. Conventionally trained surrogates can reproduce that provenance, so accuracy measured against solver-generated test sets does not by itself establish fidelity to the exact evolution. The empirical Fourier symbol makes this provenance measurable, and twin-scheme differencing makes the attribution robust to architectural spectral bias. The signature is present for a local CNN, for a non-local FNO that can represent the exact evolution, and at the operator level for nonlinear Burgers. Provenance and benchmark accuracy are distinct quantities that can respond on different scales.

## D FNO representation of the advection step

The capacity claim used in Sec. 4.2 is constructive at the level of the spectral convolution. On a periodic grid, one exact step of $u _ { t } + c u _ { x } = 0$ is diagonal in the Fourier basis: $\widehat { u } _ { k } ( t + \Delta t ) =$ $e ^ { - i 2 \pi k c \Delta t / \bar { L } } \widehat { u } _ { k } ( t )$ . An FNO spectral convolution that retains all resolved modes can assign this complex multiplier to each Fourier coefficient. In a real-valued implementation, the complex multiplication is the corresponding $2 \times 2$ rotation/scaling block on the real and imaginary parts of each mode. For the full implemented network, which also contains pointwise maps and nonlinearities, this argument is a capacity guide rather than a formal exact-representation theorem; the exact-trained run in Table 2 is therefore the operative control, showing that optimization finds an essentially exact symbol when the FNO is trained on exact data.

## E Additional discussion and limitations

The diagnostic measures relative alignment with two references, the exact evolution and the training scheme. It does not decide whether a network has “learned physics” in an absolute sense: $\alpha \approx 0$ only says that the scheme’s error direction is absent from the measured symbol. The evidence here covers one-dimensional periodic advection and forced Burgers at modest resolution, two architectures and three seeds. For Burgers, the “exact” comparator is a dealiased spectral numerical reference, not a continuum oracle.

The identification argument requires B(k) to be scheme-independent, and it is only approximately so: the two corpora have different spectral content, so the optimization trajectories are not identical. This is why we rely on out-of-distribution single-mode probing and on differencing rather than on subtracting an exact-trained control, whose ${ \bar { \boldsymbol { B } } } ( { \boldsymbol { k } } )$ is target-dependent. The architecture control gives some direct evidence that the cancellation works: the CNN and the FNO have very different architectural displacements, since their exact-trained controls differ by five orders of magnitude in MSE (Table 2), yet their twin differences agree to $4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ on both axes (Table 5), which is what one expects if the quantity that survives the difference is the scheme term.

The linear result is clean in part because a convolutional network represents a local stencil almost exactly, and that is a genuine mechanism rather than an artifact: local architectures carry an inductive bias toward discretization-like operators. The FNO control shows that this mechanism is not the explanation, since removing locality does not remove the effect, and the FNO’s exact-trained run shows that the exact evolution was inside its hypothesis class the whole time.

On Burgers we measure the learned one-step operator, linearized about states drawn from a stated distribution, and we control the input distribution explicitly. We do not claim a per-mode von Neumann correspondence there, and the α values above unity on off-distribution pools should be read as extrapolation along the scheme direction rather than as a sharper measurement of imitation. The dealiased spectral Burgers solver is used as a high-fidelity numerical reference; it is not an exact continuum oracle.

Autoregressive rollout adds a scheme-independent over-smoothing that compounds with step count and dominates the attractor-level spectrum (Appendix I), consistent with reported long-rollout failures (Lippe et al., 2023). It is a distinct failure mode with its own literature; our claim about numerical provenance is a claim about the learned operator.

An emulator can in principle be more accurate than the solver that trained it (Koehler and Thuerey, 2025), which in our coordinates means $\alpha < 0$ . Our instrument reports where a given model lands, and under standard training it lands at $\alpha \approx 1$ . We do observe the negative region once: the Crank– Nicolson-trained model fine-tuned on the smallest budgets sits at $\alpha = - 0 . 0 2 1$ to −0.027 (Table 7), consistently across seeds but at a magnitude close to our resolution. We report it as an observation rather than as evidence for emulator superiority.

For one-step and short-horizon protocols scored against held-out solver output, the argument is direct: the learned operator carries the scheme’s symbol, the test targets carry the same symbol, and the score rewards the agreement, so a high score does not establish fidelity to the exact evolution. For longrollout protocols the scheme signature is not what dominates; the surrogate’s own over-smoothing does. In both cases a solver-referenced score does not decompose into a physical component and a numerical one, whereas the symbol does. Which term dominates depends on the horizon, which is why the two are reported separately.

## F Related work

Von Neumann analysis gives, for a linear scheme on a periodic grid, the exact per-mode amplification factor $G _ { \mathrm { s c h e m e } } ( k )$ , and modified-equation analysis identifies which differential operator the scheme’s leading error imitates: even-derivative terms produce amplitude error (dissipation), odd-derivative terms produce phase error (dispersion) (LeVeque, 2007). Fourier pseudospectral methods and aliasing control, including the two-thirds rule used in our Burgers reference solver, are standard numericalanalysis tools (Boyd, 2001). This literature supplies the reference objects we attribute against. It does not, by itself, identify what a learned operator has inherited from its training solver.

Neural operators such as the FNO learn solution operators from solver-generated data (Li et al., 2021); learned simulators and neural PDE solvers are commonly trained and evaluated by autoregressive comparison to numerical rollouts (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Brandstetter et al., 2022). Benchmarks such as PDEBench and PDEArena standardize this practice across datasets and architectures (Takamoto et al., 2022; Gupta and Brandstetter, 2022). These protocols make large-scale comparison possible, but their targets are numerical outputs, not separate measurements of exact-evolution fidelity and scheme fidelity.

Neural networks fit low frequencies first and represent high frequencies poorly (Rahaman et al., 2019; Tancik et al., 2020), and the effect persists in physics-informed and operator learning (Khodakarami et al., 2026; Lippe et al., 2023). This is a property of the architecture and optimization rather than of the training targets, which is precisely what makes it a confound here: it displaces a learned symbol in the same direction a dissipative scheme does. Our design treats it as a nuisance term to be cancelled rather than as the effect under study.

Suriyanarayanan, Chakraborty, and Maulik (Suriyanarayanan et al., 2026) show that learned turbulence closures inherit the discretization error of the solver used to generate their training data, and condition the model on the solver to exploit this. The evidence there is aggregate: energy spectra that differ by scheme, without a per-mode amplification factor, without separating amplitude from phase error, and without a scheme-free reference operator to attribute against. Related work learns correction terms inside differentiable solvers (Um et al., 2020), learns stencil-like differential operators (Long et al., 2018), or combines low- and high-fidelity operator data (Howard et al., 2023). Koehler and Thuerey analyze theoretically when an emulator can be more accurate than the low-fidelity solver that trained it, the regime we label $\alpha < 0$ , but assess trained networks through aggregate error and rollout rather than through their learned symbols. Our question is complementary: after ordinary supervised training on a fixed solver corpus, where does the learned one-step operator lie relative to the exact reference and to the training scheme?

Gao et al. (2026) extract the per-mode gain and phase of a trained operator network’s Jacobian and compare them to the exact advection symbol. We adopt that measurement primitive. The difference is what the symbol is compared against: they compare to the exact operator only, and so measure error, whereas we compare to the exact operator and to the training scheme, label the two error directions, and difference two schemes to remove the architectural contribution. Among the closest diagnostics and solver-aware methods above, we are not aware of one that attributes a learned operator to a specific training discretization by comparing its Fourier response to both the exact and scheme references.

Long autoregressive rollouts of neural PDE surrogates degrade in characteristic ways, and correcting that degradation is an active area (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Brandstetter et al., 2022; Lippe et al., 2023). Noise injection, pushforward training and refinement-based methods all target the distribution shift and frequency loss that appear when a one-step model is used autoregressively. Our rollout measurements are consistent with this literature and are the reason we report provenance on the one-step operator: rollout introduces a distortion that belongs to the surrogate rather than to either reference operator, and it can dominate the attractor-level spectrum.

## G Additional linear-advection checks

Deliberately restricting the surrogate (small width, few epochs, red-spectrum training data) to induce a larger architectural displacement leaves the twin-scheme difference essentially unchanged (radial −0.240, tangential −0.686), whereas subtracting an exact-trained control is fragile because B(k) depends on the training target. Twin differencing is therefore the estimator we rely on. The restricted scheme-trained surrogates themselves remain faithful to their schemes, because upwind and Crank– Nicolson are local finite-stencil operators that a convolutional network represents easily (Long et al., 2018; Brandstetter et al., 2022). The exact-trained control is the run that degrades, and it degrades at full capacity too, plateauing four orders of magnitude above the scheme corpora (Table 2).

## I Rollout: a second, scheme-independent distortion

The measurements in the main paper are all one-step. The question here is whether the same signature is visible when the surrogate is run autoregressively to its own statistical steady state, which is how surrogates are often assessed.

It is not. Rolled out autoregressively, all three Burgers surrogates over-damp: their high-k spectral slopes fall to between −7.4 and −7.9 against solver slopes of −5.1 to −6.7, their high-k energy lies below that of every solver, and the ordering by scheme visible in the solvers is lost (Figure 3). This over-smoothing is scheme-independent and compounds with the number of steps, and at the attractor it dominates the scheme signature that Sec. 4.3 finds in the same models’ one-step operators.

The scope of the diagnostic follows from this. Numerical provenance is a property of the learned operator and must be measured there; an attractor-level spectrum mixes it with a distortion that belongs to the surrogate’s autoregressive use rather than to either reference operator. This is also why we report the operator and the rollout separately rather than combining them into a single score.

## J High-accuracy fine-tuning: provenance and accuracy respond on different scales

The practical question the diagnostic raises is what it would take to move a surrogate’s provenance off its training scheme. We train to convergence on a scheme corpus, so that $\alpha \approx 1$ at the start, then fine-tune on a budget of exact-evolution samples, sweeping the budget, and re-measure the symbol. Because α is read from the symbol rather than from the loss, it reports which of the two references the model is converging to, which the loss curve alone does not.

Provenance changes at the smallest budget tested: 25 exact samples, under one percent of the original training set, take α from 1.000 to 0.008 for the upwind-trained model and −0.021 for the Crank–Nicolson-trained model, and every larger budget leaves it there. Accuracy does not follow. Error against the exact evolution improves but saturates at $3 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ which is the floor the exact-trained CNN reaches in Table 2 and is therefore a property of the architecture rather than of the data budget. The two coordinates come apart: α reports that the surrogate no longer carries its solver’s error direction, while the loss reports that it still cannot represent the exact operator. Neither implies the other. We state this as a result about this setting, in which a small high-fidelity budget changed provenance and the loss curve alone would not have shown it, rather than as a general claim that 25 samples remove inherited numerics.

## M Numerical reproducibility notes

Two implementation issues produced silently incorrect results during this work and are recorded here because both yield plausible output rather than an error.

Our first FNO runs did not train, stalling just below the loss of the identity map. The cause was not the model but the backward pass through a complex-valued einsum on the MPS backend, which we measured to be wrong by $\sim 1 4 0 \%$ relative error in $\partial \mathcal { L } / \partial W$ against a CPU reference, while $\mathtt { r f f t } / \mathrm { i r f f t }$ autograd agreed to $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 }$ . Rewriting the mode-wise multiplication in real arithmetic restores agreement with the CPU reference to $4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 }$ . The failure mode matters for the conclusions of Sec. $\bar { 4 . 2 } \mathrm { : }$ an untrained FNO can produce a conclusion that is numerically plausible and scientifically incorrect, namely that non-local architectures do not reproduce their training scheme. Any new architecture here is checked against the identity-map loss before its symbols are read.
